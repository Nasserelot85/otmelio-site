Markup isn't margin.
Know what your next job will really earn.

A practical guide to building estimates, pricing for a target margin, and tracking what changes between the quote and the final bill.

See the pricing example

Winning a job is not the same as earning a profit. For independent contractors, three numbers tell different stories: the amount you charge, the amount the job costs, and the amount left after those costs.

1. Markup and gross margin are not the same

Markup compares added profit to cost. Gross margin compares gross profit to the selling price. Mixing them up can make a quote look more profitable than it is.

ExampleAmount
Estimated job cost$100.00
25% markup on cost$25.00
Selling price$125.00
Gross profit$25.00
Gross margin20%

To achieve a 25% gross margin on the same $100 job cost, the target selling price is $100 ÷ (1 − 0.25) = $133.33 (rounded). Gross margin is calculated as (selling price − job cost) ÷ selling price.

Pricing reminder: Decide which costs are included before calculating margin. A gross margin on direct job costs is not the same as net business profit after office overhead, financing, taxes, and other expenses.

2. Build the quote from your own costs

Generic industry averages are rarely precise enough for an individual job. Before sending a quote, make a working estimate of:

  • Materials and current supplier prices, including expected waste
  • Labor hours, pay rates, and subcontractor costs
  • Equipment hire, travel, permits, and job-specific expenses
  • An explicit overhead allowance and desired margin
  • What is included, excluded, and subject to a written change order

3. Track approved changes separately

Unrecorded scope changes can turn a reasonable quote into a weak result. Log each requested change, its estimated impact, the customer's approval, and the updated job total. Don't silently treat unapproved work as collectible revenue.

4. Compare estimated costs with actual costs

After the work is complete, compare the estimate against actual material invoices, labor hours, and other job expenses. Record what was billed and paid. Use the difference to improve the next quote, rather than assuming that a healthy-looking invoice means a healthy margin.

5. A quick pre-quote checklist

  1. Have I used current costs for this specific job?
  2. Have I separated markup from target gross margin?
  3. Have I included realistic labor time and job overhead assumptions?
  4. Are the scope, exclusions, and change-order process clear?
  5. Can I compare the final job result to the original estimate?
